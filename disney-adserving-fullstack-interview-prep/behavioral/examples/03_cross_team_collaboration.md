# STAR template: cross-team / third-party integration

Example prompt shape: "tell me about a time you had to work across teams to get
something done" / "describe integrating with a system you didn't own" / "tell me about
a disagreement with another team about an API/contract."

JD language this maps to: *"a willingness and desire to effectively communicate and
collaborate across teams and systems"* plus explicit responsibilities integrating with
"entitlements," "pacing," "targeting," and "third-party systems" — this role sits at the
intersection of several other teams' services by design, so this isn't a soft-skills
throwaway question, it's asking whether you can operate in that topology.

- **Situation**: _(what was the integration — another internal team's service, or an
  external third party — and why did your work depend on it?)_
- **Task**: _(what were you trying to get done, and what made the boundary hard —
  unclear ownership, a contract/API that didn't fit your use case, conflicting
  priorities/timelines with the other team?)_
- **Action**: _(how you resolved it — did you propose a contract change and negotiate
  it, build a defensive layer against an API you couldn't change, escalate, or just
  invest time understanding their constraints before proposing a fix? Specifics of *how*
  you communicated matter more than the technical fix itself here.)_
- **Result**: _(did the integration ship; is the relationship/process better now than
  before — e.g., a shared contract test, a regular sync, a documented SLA?)_
- **Learning**: _(what you now do differently the moment you know a project touches a
  system you don't own — e.g., "I loop in the owning team during design, not after I've
  already built against my assumption of their API.")_

## Common failure mode to avoid

Don't tell a story where the "resolution" is just "I was right and they eventually
agreed" — even if true, that reads as combative rather than collaborative unless you can
also point to what you did to understand *their* constraints first. The strongest
version of this story shows you changing your own approach based on something you
learned from the other team, not just convincing them of your original plan.
