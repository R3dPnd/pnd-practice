# Behavioral — prep notes

Unlike Microsoft's explicit four-theme rubric, Disney doesn't publish a formal
behavioral scorecard — but its own careers site (see `../README.md`'s Field notes for
the source) gives real, specific guidance, and it's worth taking at face value rather
than guessing.

## The structure Disney itself recommends: STAR (+ an optional Learning beat)

Disney's own interview-prep page recommends the classic **STAR** framework, in
explicitly storytelling language:

- **Situation**: "Set the stage for your interviewer" — where were you, what was
  happening, why did it matter. 1–2 sentences, enough for a stranger to follow.
- **Task**: the challenge or goal you were specifically responsible for.
- **Action**: "the specific steps you took, the creativity you brought, and how you
  collaborated with others." First person — what *you* did, not just what "the team" did.
- **Result**: the outcome and impact of your efforts, ideally with a number or concrete
  artifact.

Disney's own material stops there — it doesn't ask for a closing "what I'd change"
line the way Microsoft explicitly does. **Add one anyway.** A concrete "here's what I
do differently now" sentence is a strictly stronger answer under any rubric, costs you
one sentence, and no interviewer penalizes a beat they didn't ask for:

- **Learning** *(your addition, not Disney's requirement)*: the specific behavioral
  change you made afterward — what you'd do differently, or did differently next time.

## Themes to prepare stories for

No official four-theme list like Microsoft's, but independent reports and Disney's own
framing converge on these. Prepare at least one story per theme; a good story often
covers two at once.

1. **Storytelling / ownership of your journey** — Disney's own material explicitly
   frames your career as a narrative where you "drive the plot." This is really an
   ownership question wearing different language: did you drive something to
   completion, including the unglamorous parts, without being told to — and can you
   narrate *why* you made the choices you made, not just what happened.
2. **Collaboration / teamwork** — reported prompts ask about working across teams,
   resolving disagreement, and "going above and beyond" for a teammate or partner team.
3. **Guest/customer impact** — Disney's whole cultural identity (the "cast member"
   mindset, explicitly referenced in candidate reports) is built around the idea that
   everyone's work eventually reaches a guest/customer. For a DEEP&T front-end role,
   this maps directly onto: does your work actually make it into something a Disney+/
   Hulu/ESPN+ viewer, or an internal editorial/ops user, experiences? Frame technical
   decisions in terms of the person on the other end, not just the internal win.
4. **Growth / handling ambiguity or setback** — a failure, a production incident, or an
   ambiguous problem you had to scope yourself. One reported real technical-round
   question was explicitly "tell me about a production incident/regret" — have a
   specific one ready, not a hypothetical.

## Day-of reminders

- Lead every answer with 1–2 sentences of Situation — don't bury the "so what" three
  paragraphs in.
- Say "I," not "we," for the Action beat — the interviewer is evaluating your specific
  contribution, not your team's.
- Close with the Learning beat even though Disney's own material doesn't explicitly
  require it — see above.
- Given the reported "conversational, not gotcha" tone, don't over-rehearse to the
  point of sounding scripted — know your beats, but let the delivery be natural.
- Since there's no dedicated "resume deep-dive" round confirmed for Disney either way,
  assume (as with Microsoft) that a technical interviewer can pause and ask about a
  specific project at any point — have these stories ready even in a coding round.
