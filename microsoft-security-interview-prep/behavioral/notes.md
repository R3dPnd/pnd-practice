# Behavioral — prep notes

Woven throughout all three rounds (see `../README.md`'s "What each round is actually
grading" and "Field notes" sections) — Microsoft doesn't run a separate resume
deep-dive round; a coding or system-design interviewer can pause and ask about a
specific project at any point.

## The STAR+L structure

**STAR** (Situation, Task, Action, Result) plus a fifth beat: **L — the concrete
behavioral change you made afterward.** That closing sentence — what you'd do
differently, or did differently next time — is reportedly the single most important
line in a Microsoft behavioral answer. Don't end on the Result.

- **Situation**: 1–2 sentences of context — enough for a stranger to follow, no more.
- **Task**: what were you specifically responsible for (not the team, you).
- **Action**: what you actually did — specific decisions, not "we worked together."
  First person, not "we."
- **Result**: the outcome, ideally with a number or concrete artifact.
- **Learning**: the one thing you changed about how you work afterward. This is what
  separates a story from a resume bullet, and it's the beat candidates most often skip.

## The four themes Microsoft behavioral questions actually probe

Not free-form — reported real prompts cluster around four recurring themes (see
`../README.md` field notes for sourcing):

1. **Growth mindset** — learning from failure/setbacks. Microsoft's culture is
   explicitly built around Carol Dweck's "growth mindset" framing since the Nadella
   era; this is not a generic "tell me about a failure" ask, it's specifically about
   what you changed after.
2. **Collaboration / cross-team friction** — reported real prompt: "describe a time
   collaborating across teams didn't go well, and your learnings."
3. **Ownership** — did you drive something to completion, including the unglamorous
   parts, without being told to.
4. **Customer impact** — did your work connect to an actual user/customer outcome,
   not just an internal technical win.

Reported real prompt: "tell me about a time you had a conflict with someone — how did
you resolve it and what did you learn."

## Day-of reminders

- Prepare 4–5 stories total, but map each one to *at least two* themes if you can —
  you likely won't get to use all 5, and a good ownership story often also shows
  growth mindset or customer impact when asked a follow-up.
- Never end on the Result — always land on the Learning beat.
- Be ready for an interviewer to interrupt a coding or design round with "tell me
  about a time..." — don't treat behavioral prep as scoped to one round.
- If asked "what would you do differently," a real, specific answer beats a vague
  "communicate more" — say what you'd literally do differently next time.
- Practice saying these out loud, not just reading them — the STAR+L shape falls
  apart under interview nerves if you've only ever read it silently.
